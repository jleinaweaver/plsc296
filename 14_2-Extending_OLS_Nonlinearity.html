SLIDE: Let’s refresh our memories on evaluating the fit of an OLS regression model

Evaluating the Fit of an OLS Regression


  1. Is there a problem with missing data?

  2. Are the coefficients significant?

  3. How much of the outcome is explained by the model?

  4. Is there a problem in the residuals?

What is the concern with missing data in your regression?

  • (Missing at random vs missing not at random, aka selection bias)


What do significant coefficients tell me about your regression model?

  • (Can we reject the null hypothesis?)


How do I evaluate if my \(R^2\) value is “good” or not?

  • (It depends on what you are trying to do)

  • (If the goal is predicting outcomes, e.g. who wins an election, higher \(R^2\) is better)

  • (If the goal is developing a precise estimate of a causal effect, \(R^2\) kind of doesn’t matter)


And finally, what am I checking in the residuals?

  • What should they look like?

  • (SLIDE)

Assumptions about OLS Residuals

  1. Zero mean

  2. Constant variance (homoscedasticity)

  3. No autocorrelation

  4. Normally distributed

IFF your model fits the data well, then your residuals should have these four qualities


What does it mean to say your residuals should have zero mean?

  • (Model line should go through the middle of the data and the positive and negative residuals should offset)


What does it mean to say our errors should be homoscedastic?

  • (The spread of the error should be the same across all levels of the model)


What does it mean to say that there should be no autocorrelation in our errors?

  • (No patterns in the residuals; no correlation between them)


And finally, why should our residuals be normally distributed?

  • (Basically, if you check the other three boxes your residuals are random noise and adding together random noise should look like a normal curve)


Any questions on our four steps for evaluating a regression?


What we didn’t get to last class was what do we do if our residuals indicate a serious problem in the fit of our model

  • Today we learn two approaches dependent on the problem revealed by the residuals


Approaches:

  1. If the effect you are studying is nonlinear you can add squared and cubed terms to the regression (or more!)

  2. If your outcome variable has a strong right skew you can transform it using the log

Adapting OLS regressions to fit non-linear relationships

  • Exponential Growth Data
  • Version 1
  • Version 2

Example 1: Exponential growth

  • Here I have plotted some toy data that follows an exponential growth curve

  • e.g. increases begin slowly but speed up as we move across the levels of the predictor


Version 1: We can definitely fit a simple regression to this data

  • Heck, it even comes pretty close to the trend!

  • However, you can see here the pattern in the residuals without even needing to check the residuals plot


Version 2: In this form we allow X to have TWO effects on the line

  • X impacts Y both in its regular form (e.g. 1, 2, 3, 4) AND also as a square (e.g. 1, 4, 9, 16)

  • Early on the squared term has a small impact on the line and that impact gets bigger and bigger


Note that this formula for a line also allows the effect of each version of X to differ from each other!

  • Each X has a SEPARATE beta term in the regression

  • This makes this form VERY adaptable


For example, you could have a strong, positive slope on regular X but a tiny slope on X^2

  • That would be a mostly straight line that skews upward very slowly


Alternatively, you could have a small slope coefficient on X and a huge slope on the squared term

  • This is a trend that launches upward like a rocket


Do these intuitions make sense?

SLIDE: Log transformations

Original Scale

Log Scale

model2_log <- lm(data = wdi, life_expectancy ~ I(log(gdp_pc_current_usd)))

Back in Week 6 we explored how we could use a log transformation to help us analyze right skewed data


GDP per capita is clearly right skewed

  • Most countries in the world are on the poorer end of the spectrum with a few massive outliers


Think of this from the perspective of OLS

  • How do you draw a straight line through the original distribution that keeps the same amount of error on both sides?

  • But so much easier with the logged version!

Our model here suggests increasing the log value of GDP pc by one increases life expectancy by four years

  • Remind me, what does a change of one on the log scale look like in the normal scale?

  • (Multiply the amount by Euler’s number, 2.72)

  • (Rule of thumb, tripling of the base)

  • (SO, triple your GDP pc and add 4 years life expectancy on average)


The ggpredict function translates this for us back into the normal scale

  • At lower ends of the predictions the effect is fast (easy to triple a small GDP number)

  • At the high end, tripling is REALLY hard so the effect slows way down!


Have we fixed the residuals problem?


Ok, questions on our two methods for addressing nonlinearity in residuals?

  • If you suspect the effect changes direction try adding a squared or cubed term to the predictors

  • If the predictor or outcome is right-skewed try taking the log
